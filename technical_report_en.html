NOTEBOOK 01

Data and Elo system

Data source

Kaggle dataset (martj42/international-football-results-from-1872-to-2017), updated through 2026. It contains 49,287 matches, including the 72 World Cup 2026 fixture matches with no result yet.

Decision: 2010–2026 time window

Matches before 2010 are excluded for model training, for three reasons: pre-2010 football is a different process (tactics, physical level, globalization); average goals fluctuate between 1.4 and 1.7 within the window without a systematic trend (stationarity verified); and the window provides enough matches for a robust temporal split.

Important: the Elo was calculated using the full available history (from 1872), not only from 2010 onward. When a team appears in the modeling dataset in 2010, its Elo already reflects decades of prior results. The 2010–2026 window defines when we train, not from when we calculate the rating.

Custom Elo system

What is Elo and what is it for?

The Elo is a numerical rating system that measures the relative level of each team at a given point in time. It was invented by physicist Arpad Elo for chess and widely adopted in competitive sports. The central idea is simple: each team has a number that goes up when it wins and down when it loses, but the change depends on how surprising the result was.

If Spain (Elo 2100) beats Curaçao (Elo 1550), the change is minimal — that result was expected. If Curaçao beats Spain, the change is large for both teams — it was a surprise.

Why we use it: it is the most stable signal available for international football. It summarizes years of history in one number, updates automatically based on results and has demonstrated predictive power. With Elo differences above 300 points, the favorite wins between 80% and 88% of matches in our data.

Why we calculated it ourselves instead of using eloratings.net or other sources: full control over parameters (initial value, K factor), complete coverage of all teams and direct learning from the process.

Elo is updated match by match in strict chronological order, ensuring that only past information is used at the time of each match (no data leakage).

Update formula
new_elo = current_elo + K × (actual_result − expected_result) expected_result = 1 / (1 + 10^((opponent_elo − team_elo) / 400))

K factor — what does it control and why does it matter?

What is the K factor?

The K factor is the amplifier of each match’s impact on the rating. It determines how many Elo points are transferred between teams as a consequence of a result.

With a high K, the system is reactive: a surprising result changes the rating a lot immediately. With a low K, the system is stable: it requires many results to move the rating significantly.

Why we vary it by tournament: not all matches have the same informational value. A friendly says little about a team’s true level (coaches rotate players, there is no pressure). A World Cup match concentrates maximum effort and rivalry. Using one uniform K would treat both equally, contaminating the rating with weak signal.

Features and match-outcome model

Why add features on top of Elo?

Elo captures accumulated level but has two limitations: it does not capture momentum (five straight wins vs. five irregular wins can produce the same Elo) and it does not capture margin of victory (a 7–0 and a 1–0 update Elo the same way).

FeatureWhat it capturesWhat Elo does not capture
diff_eloRelative level between teamsBase feature

diff_puntos_recientesMomentum (last 5 matches)Trend, not only level
diff_goles_anotadosRecent attacking potentialMargin of victory

Critical decision: temporal split

Training: 2010–2022 (11,950 matches). Evaluation: 2023–2026 (3,442 matches). Random cross-validation is not used because time has direction: mixing dates produces artificially good metrics (data leakage).

Chosen model: Multinomial Logistic Regression

It produces probabilities directly for 3 classes (Home / Draw / Away). It is interpretable, robust with moderately sized datasets and hard to beat with weak features. Random Forest or XGBoost would require more data and stronger features to justify the added complexity.

Evaluation metrics — Log-Loss and Brier Score

Why not use accuracy?

The accuracy (percentage of correct outcomes) seems intuitive but is a poor metric for probabilistic models. A model that always predicts “the favorite wins” can have good accuracy but still be useless: it tells you nothing about uncertainty or how confident you should be in each case.

We want to know whether the probabilities are high quality: log-loss and Brier score penalize incorrect probabilities and allow model comparison. For calibration in the strict sense — checking that when the model says 30%, the event occurs close to 30% of the time — the notebook also includes calibration curves (reliability plots) by class, showing that both models are reasonably calibrated without obvious systematic bias.

Log-Loss — penalizes wrong confidence

The log-loss measures how much the model penalizes itself for wrong predictions. The formula uses logarithms, creating a penalty that grows exponentially when the model is very confident about something that did not happen.

Example: if the model says “Argentina has a 95% chance to win” and Argentina loses, log-loss is very high. If it says “Argentina has a 55% chance” and Argentina loses, log-loss is low.

Range: 0 = perfect prediction. There is no upper bound, but the naive model based on the training distribution has log-loss = 1.0554 in this split. Our full model achieved 0.8670 — a clear improvement, but more moderate than the previous naive calculation suggested.

Brier Score — squared error of probabilities

The Brier Score is the equivalent of mean squared error (MSE), but applied to probabilities. It measures the squared difference between the predicted probability and the actual outcome (0 or 1).

It is more intuitive than log-loss: if the model predicts a 0.7 win probability and the team wins, the error is (0.7 - 1)² = 0.09. If it predicted 0.3 and the team wins, the error is (0.3 - 1)² = 0.49.

Range: 0 = perfect prediction, 1 = worst possible. A model that always predicts 0.5 has Brier ≈ 0.25. Our model achieved 0.170 — clearly below the naive baseline.

NOTEBOOK 03

Goals model (Poisson)

Why a separate goals model?

The match-outcome model predicts win/draw/loss but says nothing about the scoreline. To simulate the tournament, concrete scores are needed: a 1–0 and a 3–2 are both home wins, but they have different implications for goal difference in the group stage.

Why a Poisson distribution?

What is the Poisson distribution and what does it measure?

The Poisson distribution models the probability of a given number of events occurring in a time interval, assuming events are independent and have an average occurrence rate (λ, lambda).

In football, goals are exactly that: discrete events (0, 1, 2, 3…), never negative, occurring at an average rate that depends on the teams. The average goals in our data is 1.36 goals per team per match, and the empirical distribution is compatible with a Poisson with λ ≈ 1.36 — making it reasonable as a reference model.

Why not linear regression: linear regression does not know that goals cannot be negative. It could predict -0.3 goals for a very weak team, which makes no sense. Poisson is designed for counts.

How we use it: the model estimates a lambda for each team in each match. Then we sample actual goals from a Poisson(λ) distribution. This injects realistic randomness: with λ=1.4, sometimes the result is 0, sometimes 1, sometimes 3 — just like in reality.

Model formula
λ = e^(β₀ + β₁×elo_equipo + β₂×elo_rival + β₃×es_local) # Estimated coefficients (Elo normalized /1000): elo_equipo: +2.013 # higher own Elo → more goals elo_rival: -2.279 # higher opponent Elo → fewer goals es_local: +0.249 # home team scores 28% more than away team

Known limitation: the independent Poisson model slightly underestimates zeros and overestimates ones. In V2, the Dixon-Coles model could be explored; it adds a correlation term to correct this bias in tight results (0–0, 1–0, 0–1, 1–1).
NOTEBOOK 04

Monte Carlo tournament simulation

What is Monte Carlo simulation?

Monte Carlo — massive repetition of random scenarios

Monte Carlo simulation is a technique that solves complex problems by repeating a random experiment thousands of times and observing the distribution of outcomes. The name comes from the Monte Carlo casino, a symbol of randomness.

Football is too complex to solve analytically: 48 teams, 12 groups, thousands of possible knockout brackets, and each match has three possible outcomes. It is impossible to directly calculate “what is the probability that Argentina and Spain meet in the semifinals?”

The Monte Carlo solution: instead of calculating, we simulate. We run the full tournament 10,000 times. In each simulation, each match is resolved by sampling a random result according to the model probabilities. At the end, we count frequencies: if Argentina won the tournament in 1,212 out of 10,000 simulations, its estimated probability is 12.12%.

Why 10,000? With few simulations (100), estimates are noisy. With 10,000, variance is low enough that 1–2% differences between teams are statistically stable. For this level of precision, 10,000 is the balance point between quality and compute time.

Simulated tournament structure

StageDetail
Groups12 groups of 4 teams · 6 matches per group · 72 total matches
QualificationTop 2 from each group (24) + 8 best third-place teams = 32 qualifiers
Round of 32 → Final32 teams · official FIFA 2026 matchups (exact Annex C, M73–M88) + best-third-place assignment. Round of 16 → Final: official M89–M104 progression
Round of 16 → Final4 additional knockout rounds
DrawsPenalties 50/50 throughout the knockout stage
Simulations10,000 tournaments through the final, excluding the third-place match

What do the numbers mean?

The probabilities are not predictions: they are relative frequencies across 10,000 possible scenarios. Spain at 20.2% means they won in 2,020 out of 10,000 simulations. The system produces an uncertainty distribution, not a winner.
